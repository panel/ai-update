The Microscope and the Mirror

AI keeps winning at science it was never trained for — proteins, planets, a falsified conjecture — while Gemini 4 Neon quietly retakes the frontier. The counterweight: robots still can’t tell a reflection from a target.

The Big Picture

Three days ago the story was a claimed Riemann proof. This period the labs stopped treating that as a one-off. OpenAI published evidence that GPT-6 models have intrinsic protein-design capabilities — binding observed across five targets, not via a bespoke AlphaFold-style system but through general reasoning and code. The same week, amateurs used Codex and Astra to surface candidate exoplanets in public Kepler/TESS data, a professor watched GPT-6.2 Astra solve a 1808 archival mystery in 22 minutes, and DeepMind paired agent teams with formal verification to find faster quantum algorithms. The through-line we named last week has hardened: frontier models are now general-purpose scientific instruments, and the interesting work is pointing them at real data.

The sharper jolt is competitive. Gemini 4 Neon marks Google DeepMind’s full return as a frontier lab — it beats Astra and Fable on LiveBench and cut one real Rust benchmark’s runtime by nearly half. Independent benchers agree: Lenny’s 25-task eval now ranks Neon top, with GPT-6.2 a disappointment and Opus 5.5 the “least annoying daily driver.” If you locked your toolchain to one lab this year, this is your reminder that the lead keeps changing hands on a weeks-long clock.

And then the thing you’d regret skipping as noise: a Reddit thread at 400+ points claiming a lab may have cracked integer factoring in P. Unconfirmed, almost certainly overcooked — but filed alongside OpenAI’s own math repo quietly hosting a preprint falsifying the polynomial Hirsch conjecture, it tells you where the frontier’s attention has gone. Watch this one; don’t trade on it.

Themes

AI as a scientific instrument (update)

We introduced “mathematics is the new benchmark” on Saturday. The update is that the lane widened from math to the physical and historical sciences in 72 hours. The pattern across the protein, exoplanet, and quantum-algorithm results is the same architecture: a general reasoning model, plus code execution, plus a verification loop — formal proof, lab assay, or archival confirmation — closing the gap between “plausible” and “true.” DeepMind’s mass-spec and quantum work both lean on verification as the discipline that makes autonomy trustworthy. The Astra historical find is the most instructive for non-scientists: it didn’t just retrieve, it predicted a document existed in a paper archive and said examining it would confirm the hypothesis. That’s hypothesis-generation, not search.

The looped transformer shows up twice

Architecturally, the quiet story is weight-shared iteration. GLM-5.4 is Z.ai’s first loop transformer — a group of layers runs several times per pass, buying depth without parameters — and it’s the second such model in a week, after Thinking Machines’ Inkling 2 Mini runs its middle layers three times per token. Both pitch the same benefit: more reasoning per token, which matters most on agentic coding where answer quality compounds. Two independent labs converging on looping in the same week is a signal worth tracking; if it holds, it’s a cheaper path to “thinking harder” than scaling parameters or burning reasoning tokens. GLM-5.4 isn’t open weights yet (safety-hardening first), but Inkling 2 Mini is already being hill-climbed on a single RTX PRO 6000.

The embodiment gap is real — and so is laziness

For all the scientific wins, the agent research this period is a cold shower on the physical and the patient. ICLWorld runs the same frontier agents on computers and robots: Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra hit 82%/76% on computer-use but collapse to ~27% on robot-use from the same in-context rules. RoboDojo-Mirror is more damning — a plain mirror drops manipulation success by up to 62 points, with policies “phantom-targeting” reflections. And RLVB (87 upvotes) names a failure mode you’ve felt: agents commit to an answer before the evidence supports it — “lazy” guessing that outcome rewards can’t fix, because a lucky guess scores like a reasoned one. The fix is rewarding the agent for continuing to gather evidence while its own posterior is still uncertain. Useful mental model for anyone designing agent loops: reward the process of knowing, not just the final answer.

The provenance backlash hardens into policy

The cultural immune response is moving from grumbling to rules. Tidal now bans AI-generated music outright (334 points), and the NYT is telling photographers not to use the latest Photoshop over provenance concerns. Underneath the culture war is a real infrastructure story: AI supply-chain security is now a canary-token discipline, and the climate cost is getting loud enough that OpenAI itself published its pretraining loss curve and proposed a shared training-compute limit — a striking move for a frontier lab to make in public. For developers, the actionable bit: provenance and content-origin metadata are becoming a product requirement, not a nice-to-have.

Personal agents grow a computer

The agent-orchestration layer is maturing from chat to standing operations. The breakout is n8n’s nod — an open-source personal agent with its own computer that chases invoices, cancels trials on real websites, and asks before acting, running locally on the model you choose. That “its own computer, asks before it acts” framing is becoming the default shape. Every’s ops lead describes running seven parallel lines of work in 29 minutes via an orchestrator spinning up subthreads — and candidly flags the cognitive load that creates. The question is shifting from “can the agent multitask” to “can you supervise the fan-out.”
