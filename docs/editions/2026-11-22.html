The Proof Machine

A claimed proof of the Riemann Hypothesis — one human, one with Claude — lands in the same week Anthropic becomes the largest IPO in history and the market promptly swoons. The through-line: AI stopped writing code and started doing science.

The Big Picture

For two years the interesting question about frontier models was whether they could do your job. This week it quietly became whether they can do mathematics nobody has done. The headline is almost too big to parse: a proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis appears to have arrived twice, independently — once from Alessandro Masullo, all human, with Brian Conrad vetting it for months, and once from Levent Alpöge working with Claude, formalized without assumptions. Treat both as claims under review, not settled results. But the shape of the moment is unmistakable, and Alpöge’s own account — “Claude far outpaced me near the finish line… Claude flew, and danced, and sparkled” — is the kind of sentence that recalibrates what you think these tools are for.

It didn’t happen in a vacuum. The same period gave us formal verification emerging as the serious weapon against AI slop — Lean plus LLMs automating proofs that used to need specialists — an AI-discovered, AI-verified CPU architecture that topped Hacker News, and OpenAI’s own data on coding agents accelerating its research. The texture of “AI as a research collaborator” went from aspiration to artifact in about a week.

Meanwhile the money got real and then got nervous. Anthropic closed the largest IPO on record — $83 at pricing, $111.50 at the open, a ~$1.5–2T valuation — and within 48 hours the Nasdaq had erased its entire 2026 gain in an AI-led sell-off. The public markets now own a piece of the frontier, with all the volatility that implies.

So the mental shift for the week: the ceiling moved. If the machine can credibly contribute to Millennium-Prize-adjacent math and silicon design, the “can it review its own PRs” conversation from last week starts to feel like the floor of a much taller building.

Themes

Mathematics is the new benchmark

The Riemann story matters less as a result (still unverified) than as a category change. We’ve spent a year watching models saturate coding evals; now the frontier is problems where there is no training-set answer to retrieve. The two independent GRH drafts are the vivid case, but the connective tissue is formalization: Latent Space’s piece on Lean-assisted formal verification argues that the way you trust an AI-generated proof — or AI-generated code — is to mechanically check it, and that this is finally becoming automatable. That same logic is why PostgreSQL 19 shipped two months late, buried under an “AI-reported vulnerability apocalypse”: models that can find real bugs at scale also generate real work at scale. Verification, not generation, is becoming the bottleneck — and the thing worth building around.

The takeoff conversation gets uncomfortably concrete

Timelines are the subtext of everything above, and this week the people who think hard about them got louder. OpenAI published data showing its researchers’ AI usage growing exponentially — the top decile burning ~$25k of tokens a day — and argued all labs should be required to publicly track how much AI is accelerating their own work, a tacit admission that recursive self-improvement is now a measurable quantity. On the alarmed end, Paul Christiano told Dwarkesh there’s “meaningful risk of AI taking over within the next 6–12 months,” and Zvi has people reassessing timelines off the back of the METR report. Calibrate this as contested, not consensus — IEEE Spectrum ran a sharp “the superintelligence debate has serious flaws” counter — but the Overton window on “how fast” visibly shifted this week.

Agents got wallets — and the predators arrived

ChatGPT shipped a wallet and the practical question is instantly “what do you actually let it buy.” But the more instructive signal is the immune response. Claire Vo’s ChatPRD is drowning in token fraud — organized rings looping free trials to resell inference, in one case to power a Roblox map studio — and she’s killing free trials entirely. (Delightful detail: the scammers probe which underlying model they’ve tunneled into using pelican-on-a-bicycle SVGs.) On the defensive side, Signal is now blocking OS-level AI agents from screen-capturing conversations, and Europe floated Pandex, an attempt to express AI-agent law over MCP. The agent-commerce layer is being built and attacked simultaneously; if you’re shipping anything agent-facing, abuse modeling is now day-one work, not a later hardening pass.

Show, don’t tell: skills beat prompts

A small but durable workflow shift is crystallizing. Every’s teardown of getting AI to write like you lands on the counterintuitive rule: stop describing your style, test drafts against your published work — and a style skill tuned to a weaker model can beat a stronger model running a generic one. That dovetails with Hugging Face’s harness adapters, which adapt a model to your harness to claw back performance and cost, and with Every now shipping skill files alongside its conference talks. This is the maturation of last week’s “post-train it cheaply” theme into something you don’t need GPUs for: portable, example-driven skill artifacts that encode taste. Worth building a personal library of.

Don’t Miss

  • AI is quietly homogenizing thought. An HN-trending experiment shows AI gives nearly everyone the same advice, and a sharp paper finds peer-review critiques converging in the LLM era — overlap at ICLR rose from 12% to 21% year-on-year, with LLM-flagged reviews measurably less distinct. The mode-collapse of human output is a cost worth watching in your own work.
  • The backlash has a voice now. Google’s own workers petitioned the company not to punish schools that reject AI (283 points), and the bluntly titled Fuck Your AI Safeguards (616 points) captured the developer frustration with over-eager refusals. Sentiment is a real variable now.
